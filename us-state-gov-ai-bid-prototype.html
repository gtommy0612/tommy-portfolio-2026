02 Challenge

The agency asked for a design proposal. I wanted ours to be the first draft of the product. A first contract with a new state agency, and a way into its other departments. I heard about the bid with the deadline already close. The agency sent PDFs, and nobody on the team had worked in this domain.

Send a design proposal in 48 hours

The agency's brief. It had to show we understood a five-party permit process we had only read about.

Make it something we could build from

Not in the brief; my own aim. A design proposal usually stops mattering once the bid is decided. I wanted a file the agency could click through, and the starting point for delivery if we won.

03 Solution

04 Research

No interviews and no process audit: the business rules existed only in the agency's documents. Claude pulled the roles and rules out of the PDFs. I checked what it produced against the documents myself before drawing any screen.

Reading the documents this way defined three problems to solve.

1 · Five parties act in a fixed order

An applicant passes a sheriff's check, then a required course, then buys from a dealer, while the agency oversees and reviews. The rules for that order were spread across PDFs.

2 · Static screens took 5 days and lost

Our last bid of similar size was 5 days of Figma concept work, and we didn't win it. This one was due in 48 hours.

3 · Applicants wait on offices they never see

An application passes through three outside offices, and the applicant has to work out where it is and what to do next.

05 Design

Key decision 1: Map how the five parties connect before drawing any screen

Problem 1
Five parties act in a fixed order, and the rules for that order existed only in PDFs.
What I changed
Lay out how the roles relate and the applicant's full path first, then split it into each role's screens. Only once the role map and the order held up did I plan each role's detailed flow.
Instead of
Drawing each role's screens straight from the documents.
Why this way
One step depends on another. After the sheriff approves, the applicant takes a basic or an extended course, depending on the training history they gave when they applied.

Key decision 2: Send clickable HTML, not static Figma screens

Problem 2
Static screens took 5 days last time and didn't win. This proposal, for a domain we had just met, was due in 48 hours.
What I changed
A clickable HTML prototype built with Claude Code from my flows, on tokens generated from our existing design system.
Why this way
A working file shows the client what we understood, and it is something we can build from if we win. The company also wanted to adopt an AI development workflow.
Trade-off
Happy path only, for every outside role from applicant to dealer. Left out: the sheriff withdrawing an approval, the agency's review of training records, reports and settings. I agreed that scope with our US PM and leadership before building.

Key decision 3: Give the applicant one page that answers "where am I?"

Problem 3
An applicant waits on three offices they never see.
What I changed
Make the application itself the map: review results, course records, downloadable proof and the next step on one page.
Instead of
Leaving applicants to work out their status and next step from the application's detail tabs.
Why this way
Wherever the application is, the applicant can see who has it and what they need to do next.

06 Validation

Clicked through as every role

I went through each version myself as every role and adjusted it until it did what the documents required.

Not checked with the agency before the bid

We had PDFs only and no access to users. The rules were checked against the documents, and checking them with the agency came after the win.

What that gap missed

I assumed a reviewed application locks. It seemed safe: the training history was part of the application, and the sheriff had already approved it.

When we started building, the agency told us otherwise. Personal details lock after review, but the training section stays editable. In the interface the fix was small, a change in which fields are read-only. But it was exactly the kind of rule I couldn't confirm in 48 hours, and the prototype gave the agency something concrete to correct.

Reflection

Speed moved the risk. It didn't remove it. AI got me from documents to a working prototype in two days, but some of the rules in it were guesses, and nobody could tell which. The training-record rule was one of them.

Next time I'd send an assumption list with the prototype: the rules we weren't sure of, written as questions for the agency. Our guesses would become questions for the client before kickoff, not surprises during the build.
